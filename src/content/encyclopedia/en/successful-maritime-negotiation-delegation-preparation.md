Many maritime delegations treat international meetings, especially in the technical committees of the International Maritime Organization (IMO) or in bilateral transport negotiations, as conversational and reactive events. Entering a negotiation without a unified organisational position and without clear limits of authority hands the initiative to the other parties at critical moments and exposes sovereign and commercial interests to costly trade-offs.

## 1. The pillars of strategic preparation: from mandate to BATNA and red lines

According to modern principles of regulatory diplomacy, senior negotiators should settle three key pillars before facing foreign parties:

- **The negotiating mandate:** precise definition of the authority delegated by the government or the senior maritime authority to take decisions, accept commitments or refer a matter back to the capital, consistent with internal rules and the Rules of Procedure of IMO meetings.
- **Stakeholder mapping:** an assessment of declared positions, hidden interests, regional coalitions and the likely alignment of votes among flag, coastal and port States.
- **Objectives, red lines and the best alternative to a negotiated agreement (BATNA):** separating "aspirational goals" from the "minimum acceptable concession" and defining precisely the no-agreement scenario to avoid unwanted compromises.

## 2. An evidence-based briefing book and a clear division of roles

A comprehensive briefing book prevents delegation members from contradicting one another. It contains the approved positions, technical arguments, documentary background and responses to likely scenarios. A clear division of roles between the head of delegation (spokesperson and decision manager), the technical expert and the legal adviser also prevents mixed signals.

_A hypothetical example of a briefing note summary before an IMO committee session (a training scenario only):_

- **Subject:** consideration of technical measures to reduce carbon intensity in an MEPC working group.
- **Delegation's approved position:** support for a technical reward mechanism, provided that no heavy financial charges are imposed on ships in regional coastal services.
- **Red line:** any requirement for early scrapping of active ships under 5,000 gross tonnage without a five-year grace period is unacceptable.
- **Alternative (BATNA):** build convergence with a bloc of like-minded States to request that the text be referred to a correspondence group and the final vote postponed.

## Three management actions before sending a negotiating delegation

To ensure an effective international presence, senior managers should put three actions in place:

1. **Approve the agenda and authority in writing:** does the head of delegation know clearly the legal limits for concessions and the conditions for breaking off negotiations?
2. **Simulate the other parties' positions:** have the negotiating parties' arguments and compromise options been rehearsed in internal hypothetical scenarios?
3. **Prepare back-office lines of communication:** is there a mechanism for coordination and real-time exchange of information between the delegation at the meeting and the expert team at home?

## Management conclusion

A maritime negotiating delegation that is not equipped with technical evidence, an approved mandate and risk-containment scenarios will be forced to give way in the turbulence of conflicting diplomatic interests. Success in a negotiation is secured before entering the conference hall.
