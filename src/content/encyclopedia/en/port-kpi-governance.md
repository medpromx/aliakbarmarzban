## The problem of undefined indicators
Two ports may measure "ship time in port" with different start and end points. Comparing such numbers, even with precise charts, is misleading. Designing a dashboard first needs a **data dictionary**: the definition of the indicator, the unit, the source of the record, the measurement period, the person responsible for data quality and the limits of interpretation.

## Four layers of performance
The **operational** layer covers waiting time, operation time and equipment productivity. The **service and trade** layer measures delays in cargo delivery, predictability of service and the quality of communication with users. The **financial and investment** layer looks at revenue sustainability, maintenance cost and delivery of committed investment. The **governance** layer reflects tariff transparency, responsiveness to complaints, compliance with safety standards and the quality of concession oversight.

## Outcome indicator or driver?
For example, long waiting times may result from peak demand, draught restrictions, lack of equipment, weak coordination with customs or weather conditions. A manager should see the outcome indicator alongside the explanatory factor. International rankings such as the CPPI are useful for discussion but, because port structures differ, they should not replace causal analysis at port level.

## Designing a management dashboard
Start with 8 to 12 core indicators and set a warning threshold and a related decision for each. The report table should show which deviations are under the operator's control, which are under the port authority's control and which need inter-agency coordination. The final output is not just a display of performance; it is **naming who is responsible for correction**.
