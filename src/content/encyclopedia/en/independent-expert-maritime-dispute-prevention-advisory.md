In commercial and contractual maritime disputes, from cargo damage, collision and demurrage claims to disputes over terminal operations and shipbuilding contracts, the parties tend to enter the conflict relying on one-sided arguments. Without an independent, specialist assessment of the technical facts, legal advisers steer the case towards costly courts or arbitral tribunals on unverified assumptions, whereas the early involvement of an impartial expert can map out a realistic path to settlement or to narrowing the dispute.

## 1. Distinguishing the specialist roles in maritime dispute resolution

To use expertise properly in practice, five professional mechanisms must be kept apart:

- **Industry advisory:** technical analysis for one party to understand its position and develop commercial positions, without any claim to formal impartiality;
- **Expert determination:** referring a defined technical or accounting question to an expert, by prior agreement of the parties, for a final and contractually binding opinion;
- **Mediation:** a facilitative, non-binding process in which an impartial specialist helps the parties reach a mutually acceptable agreement through dialogue;
- **Commercial arbitration:** an adversarial, binding process before an independent arbitral tribunal that ends in a final legal award;
- **Expert witness:** a specialist role in which the expert provides an independent expert report and defends it, regarding their fundamental duty as impartial assistance to the court or tribunal, not advocacy for the party that pays their fee.

## 2. Tracing the evidence and neutralising conflicts of interest

Under international standards for expert evidence (such as the rules of the Academy of Experts and of maritime arbitration bodies), the value of an expert's report lies in absolute impartiality and a documented methodology:

- **Evidence trails:** careful matching of voyage data recorder (VDR) data, engine and deck logbooks and contemporaneous contract correspondence against the claims made;
- **Managing conflicts of interest and the limits of competence:** an independent expert must disclose any previous working relationship and decline to give opinions in areas outside their technical expertise;
- **Exposing inconsistencies:** making gaps in the evidence clear keeps weak cases out of arbitrations costing hundreds of thousands of dollars.

## Three strategic questions for senior managers facing a dispute

Before deciding to bring a formal claim or refer a matter to arbitration, the board should assess three questions:

1. **Has the basis of the technical claims, the losses and the financial calculations been audited by an independent, impartial expert?**
2. **Has the expert's mandate been defined clearly enough for the report to be free of organisational bias?**
3. **Have the results of the evidence review and the gaps in the case documents been considered as a basis for early negotiation and commercial settlement?**

## Management conclusion

Independent expertise in maritime disputes is a defensive tool against wasting organisational resources. An expert with the courage to state the weaknesses in a case's evidence saves managers from the risk of losing in court and helps protect the company's commercial reputation.
