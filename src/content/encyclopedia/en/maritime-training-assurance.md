## What change is a course supposed to make?
A maritime management and governance programme is valuable when, after the course, a manager can analyse a real problem, assess the options and produce an output for decision-making. Showing slides or counting attendance hours is not evidence that learning has happened. Design based on **constructive alignment** links three components: the learning outcome, the learning activity and the method of assessment.

## Observable outputs
For example, in training on the III Code a suitable output is a "gap matrix and roadmap"; in port governance, a "map of decision rights and KPIs"; and in negotiation, a "delegation briefing book". Assessment should check whether participants can really produce and defend these documents. Bloom's taxonomy helps in designing objectives that go beyond memorising information.

## Quality control of sources
Every training pack should have a reference and version, a source document, a legal status, a review date and an academic owner. In regulatory subjects, distinguishing a binding decision from recommended guidance is part of training quality. A hypothetical case study must also be clearly labelled as hypothetical so that it is not mistaken for a real case.

## The framework of the full programme
In the training documents provided, a structure of **12 courses and 56 sessions, 112 hours in total** has been designed. This structure is for executive training and should not be confused with a seafarer's certificate of competency or a course officially approved by IMO. It is suggested that each course include a decision-based project and peer review as well as a knowledge test.
