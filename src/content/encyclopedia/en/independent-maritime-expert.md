## Independence before expertise
In port or shipping disputes, a technical and management report has value only when the scope of the mandate and any possible conflict of interest are clear from the start. The expert must state which issues they are giving an opinion on, what information they have reviewed and which questions fall outside their competence. Independent analysis must not turn into a rewrite of one party's position.

## A defensible report structure
A report is best opened with the "issues in question", and should then separate the documents, the timeline, the method of analysis, the assumptions, the limitations and the conclusion on each issue. Every conclusion should refer to an identifiable document. Separating **established fact** from **the parties' claims** and **expert inference** is the key to a report that can be checked.

## A Scott schedule and a timeline
In a dispute over late delivery of a terminal, a schedule can show what the employer has claimed, how the contractor has responded, which documents support each position and what data is still missing. A timeline of correspondence, notices, changes and operational events prevents coincidence in time from being mistaken for causation.

## Better dispute prevention
Many disputes start from the lack of a clear definition of delivery, an obligation to record data or a change control process. An expert review before signing, or at the first sign of deviation, can document the points of ambiguity and turn them into a corrective decision. Final legal opinions or representation in arbitration must be provided by a specialist with the relevant qualification.
