## Why the name of a model is not the answer
The literature on port reform usually describes four models: the **service port**, the **tool port**, the **landlord port** and the **fully privatised port**. They differ in who owns the infrastructure and superstructure, how services are provided and who employs the labour. In practice, however, many ports have hybrid structures. Adopting the label "landlord" without clarifying decision rights, the limits of powers and the accountability mechanism is not real reform.

## Separate the roles of regulator, owner and operator
Three roles should be identified in any diagnosis. The **regulator** sets rules and protects the public interest; the **landlord or asset owner** manages land, infrastructure and property rights; and the **operator** is responsible for operational services and contractual obligations. Some of these roles may sit in a single body, but where the regulator is also a commercial competitor, safeguards against conflicts of interest become essential. Whether roles are combined or legally separated should be judged against national law, market capacity and the purpose of the reform.

## What does the landlord model solve?
In the landlord model the port authority is usually responsible for land, basic infrastructure, strategic development and oversight of concession agreements, while a private operator manages much of the equipment and terminal operations. This arrangement can attract private investment, but it only works if the concession agreement clearly sets out service levels, committed investment, maintenance quality, fair access and the handling of breaches. Conversely, a small port or one with little competition may not suit the classic landlord model.

## From operational indicators to institutional accountability
Indicators such as ship waiting time, cargo dwell time and berth or crane productivity are necessary but not enough to judge governance. Complementary indicators include the transparency of tariff setting, the time taken to handle complaints, delivery of investment commitments, publication of performance reports and the number of unresolved disputes. Each indicator needs a data definition, a measurement period and an owner, and comparisons between ports must take account of cargo mix and operating conditions.

## A suggested reform package
Before changing the organisation chart, carry out a diagnosis of roughly six to eight weeks: document the stakeholder map, decision rights, revenue flows and investment commitments; then compare two or three suitable models for legal risk, competition, transition cost and feasibility. The final product should be "the rationale for the chosen model, a transition plan and an accountability dashboard", not just a new organisation chart.

## Decision table for the four classic port models
| Model | Main feature | Focus of public oversight |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Service | Wide public role in operations and assets | Productivity, cost, transparency and service quality |
| Tool | Public infrastructure and part of the superstructure; operations by a mix of actors | Coordination of equipment and operations, division of tasks |
| Landlord | Basic assets held by the public authority; much of the operation transferred to an operator | Concession performance, investment, access and competition |
| Fully private | Ownership and operations mainly private | Competition rules, safety, environment and public interest |

This classification is an analytical tool, not a prescription to apply identically to every port. A port authority may use the landlord model for one terminal and a different arrangement for particular marine services. **The key question** is which body takes each costly decision, from berth development to a tariff change, and under what accountability mechanism.

## Two common mistakes in structural reform
The first mistake is to transfer operational responsibility without building the capacity to monitor the contract. The public body then steps away from operations but lacks the tools to control quality and supervise investment. The second is to leave overlapping powers between the regulator and the operator; this conflict weakens independent handling of complaints and impartial enforcement of rules. A reform report should state openly the cost and risk of the transition period, from human resources to data systems.
