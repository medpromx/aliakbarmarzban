## From showing cooperation to solving a problem
A memorandum of understanding that only stresses goodwill and exchange of experience does not necessarily create practical value. The right start is to define a specific need: inspector training, upgrading a digital process, sharing port management experience or joint research. A potential partner should then be assessed against criteria such as fit with the mission, capacity to deliver, conflicts of interest and the prospect of lasting cooperation.

## Four layers of MoU design
The first layer is the **aim and scope** of the cooperation. The second is **governance of implementation**, including focal points and a joint committee. The third is **resources and information protection**: it must be clear which costs are covered and how confidentiality is maintained. The fourth is **follow-up and termination**: the review date, the criteria for success and the rules for ending the cooperation. If the text is designed from the start to be non-binding, that intention must be reflected carefully in light of the governing law.

## A suggested six-month plan
For a training partnership, three stages can be designed: in the first month, needs assessment and agreement on the programme; in months two to four, a pilot activity; in months five and six, quality assessment and a decision on whether to continue. This timetable is only a project management example. Each activity should have an owner, a budget, acceptance criteria and documented minutes.

## Measuring results
Indicators such as the number of meetings or the signing of a document do not measure added value on their own. The degree of knowledge transfer, the quality of technical outputs, continuity of contact and completion of planned actions are better indicators. Sometimes stopping a low-impact cooperation in time is also the right governance outcome.
