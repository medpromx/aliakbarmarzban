Developing port infrastructure and terminals requires very large amounts of capital and advanced technology, beyond the borrowing capacity of public budgets. Yet turning these investments into "public value" depends on a precise understanding of the structural differences between contract models:

- **Full privatisation:** the permanent transfer of ownership of public land and assets to the private sector, which in many jurisdictions faces legal or policy restrictions;
- **Outsourcing:** short-term transfer of specific operational services (such as towage or tallying) without any substantial investment commitment from the contractor;
- **Concession:** the most widely used arrangement in the landlord port model, in which the State keeps ownership of the port land and long-term operating rights are granted to an operator in return for investment in superstructure;
- **Public-private partnership (PPP):** a comprehensive legal and financial structure in which the design, financing, construction and management of an asset are arranged on the basis of a defined sharing of risks between the public and private sectors.

## 1. The pillars of institutional bankability and a competitive tender

Private investment without a transparent tender leads to rent-seeking and monopoly. The call and evaluation process must be predictable for international and commercial financing (bankability) to be possible. If qualitative and technical evaluation criteria are sacrificed to unrealistic financial figures on bid-opening day, the project will face the risk of operator insolvency or repeated demands for contract adjustment.

## 2. Tariff regulation and objective performance measures (KPIs)

Public value is lost when a private operator uses a terminal's monopoly position to impose excessive charges on shipping lines and cargo owners. The port authority must apply an effective oversight mechanism to tariff adjustment formulas and, at the same time, make continued operation conditional on key performance indicators (KPIs) such as gross crane moves, truck turnaround time and the speed of berth allocation for ships.

## Three strategic actions for senior port decision-makers

Before signing long-term partnership and concession contracts, senior managers should check three principles:

1. **Analyse the risk allocation matrix:** have commercial risks, market swings and equipment depreciation been transferred to the private sector, while risks of waterway access and permit issue remain with the State?
2. **Establish a tariff oversight regime:** is there a fair tariff cap mechanism to prevent anti-competitive behaviour and harm to the national supply chain?
3. **Secure handback conditions:** have transparent procedures been set for preventive maintenance during the contract and for handing back infrastructure in good condition at the end of the operating period?

## Management conclusion

A port public-private partnership is not a way for the State to shed responsibility; it is a test of the maturity of public regulation. Only by combining independent legal oversight, a transparent tender and continuous measurement of performance will private capital become an engine of national economic growth.
