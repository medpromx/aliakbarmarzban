Port concession agreements and terminal operating contracts often run for 20 to 30 years. Over such a period the original assumptions cannot all come true; geopolitical swings, sudden jumps in demand, changes in the configuration of shipping lines and new environmental and decarbonisation rules confront contracts with the reality of "incomplete contracts". Yet there is a fundamental difference between a constructive adaptive correction and revisions that result from weak governance.

## 1. Separating legitimate adjustment from moral hazard

According to World Bank infrastructure studies, renegotiation is justified when it stems from external structural shocks, fundamental changes in law or emergencies beyond the parties' ability to foresee, so that the original financial balance can be restored.
By contrast, frequent and early revisions usually have their roots in aggressive bidding in the original tender, where an investor wins with unrealistic figures and then, relying on the high cost of stopping operations, demands a cut in the concession fee, a reduction in committed investment or higher tariffs. Accepting such demands without tangible justification is a clear breach of the transparency and fairness of the tender.

## 2. Contractual mechanisms and transparent decision-making

To guard against the risk of collusion and waste of public resources, the review process must be conducted "within the procedures stated in the contract":

- **Structured adjustment mechanisms:** objective economic indicators and set points in time for reassessing the financial balance.
- **A documented commercial and expert justification:** publishing the expert reasoning and recording the financial consequences of each decision for the public and for oversight bodies.
- **Protecting service levels and the public interest:** ensuring that any adjustment of the operator's obligations does not lead to lower handling capacity or unfair tariff increases for shipping lines.

## Three strategic criteria for senior managers' decisions

Before entering any renegotiation, port authorities should assess three key questions:

1. **Does the imbalance come from risks outside the operator's control and pricing, or from its own commercial miscalculation?**
2. **Do the change clauses in the current contract clearly set out the limits of authority, impartial assessment procedures and formulas for financial rebalancing?**
3. **Will the proposed new structure secure the port's operational sustainability in the long term without creating a hidden financial deficit for the State or weakening service quality?**

## Conclusion

If a port contract is renegotiated on the basis of expert evidence, a transparent process and protection of stakeholders' interests, it is a smart tool for supply chain resilience. Otherwise it will be a one-sided concession that not only undermines the governance of the contract but also destroys the trust of future investors.
