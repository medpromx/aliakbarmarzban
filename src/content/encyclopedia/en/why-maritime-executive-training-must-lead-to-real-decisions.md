Many maritime organisations, ports and shipping companies spend considerable sums each year sending managers to specialist courses and seminars. Yet in most cases, once the course is over, working practices remain untouched and there is no visible effect on how legal risks are contained, how regulations are complied with or how contracts are managed. Relying on surface measures such as participants' initial satisfaction with the class or the award of a certificate is a common governance mistake, because "attending a course" does not mean "learning", learning does not mean "professional competence", and competence alone does not guarantee "visible organisational results".

## 1. The principle of constructive alignment and transfer of learning to the workplace

According to the theory of "constructive alignment" in adult learning, learning is most effective when the learning objectives, classroom activities and assessment methods match the real-world challenges of the organisation directly. Training for senior managers should not be reduced to one-way lectures and theoretical transfer of information; it should be based on analysis of live case studies, simulation of high-risk scenarios and formative assessment, to make the transfer of learning to the workplace possible.

## 2. Abstract objectives versus practical organisational outputs

The difference between formal training and practice-oriented training can be seen in how outputs are defined:

- **A traditional abstract objective:** "Familiarise managers with new International Maritime Organization (IMO) requirements or the structure of port contracts." (Assessment method: a theory test or a record of attendance.)
- **A competence-based, output-focused objective:** "Be able to draft a 90-day roadmap for the fleet's compliance with new regulations, or prepare a register of contractual risks (risk register)." (Assessment method: review of the document produced in the workshop against objective criteria, including clear identification of change-in-law risks, separation of responsibilities and definition of corrective actions.)

When managers produce documents such as a regulatory gap assessment matrix, a stakeholder map for international negotiations or a port governance diagnosis as workshop products during training, training turns from a perk into a value-creating decision-making process.

## Three key questions for senior managers before approving training courses

Before funding or approving any training programme, maritime authorities should ask providers three strategic questions:

1. **Are the course objectives based on practical decision-making skills and on solving the organisation's real problems, or do they only cover general headings?**
2. **Does the training process include practical exercises and the production of working documents suited to the organisation's mission?**
3. **What mechanism has been designed to follow up results, assess how learning is applied and gather feedback on managers' performance after the course?**

## Management conclusion

No workshop creates organisational change by itself; but a programme that is aligned with strategic needs and challenges the manager as a real decision-maker is the smartest investment in avoiding costly governance mistakes and raising organisational resilience.
