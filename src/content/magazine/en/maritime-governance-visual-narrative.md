## Introduction: why maritime governance needs an integrated view

Picture the executive team of a national ports and maritime administration on an ordinary Monday. The legal unit is preparing for an IMO audit of the State's flag and port State functions. The IT department is behind schedule on the single window that the FAL Convention has required since January 2024. A terminal concessionaire is asking to renegotiate. And the commercial team wants to know what the EU's carbon rules will cost the shipping lines that call at the port.

Each of these files usually sits with a different department, follows a different timetable and speaks a different technical language. Yet they share a root: all of them are about how international rules become national responsibilities, operational decisions and costs. When they are managed separately, the organisation spends its energy on firefighting. When they are seen as one system, priorities become clearer and the same data, people and governance arrangements can serve several obligations at once.

The eleven infographics in the visual atlas follow that system from its legal foundations to its human capacity. This article does not repeat the encyclopedia. It asks a narrower question at each step: *what does this framework change in a leader's decisions?*

## Part 1: global rules and institutional responsibility

### Who is responsible when a ship fails?

When a ship is detained in a foreign port, the first reaction is often to blame the shipowner or the inspector. The law of the sea gives a more structured answer. The 1982 UN Convention on the Law of the Sea places primary responsibility on the flag State, gives coastal States powers that vary with distance from the coast, and allows port States to control foreign ships that call voluntarily. A detention is therefore also a signal about the flag State's oversight.

[[atlas:01]]

For a manager, the figure is a map of accountability. It shows that an administration cannot treat its flag, coastal and port State duties as one undifferentiated mandate: each has a different legal basis, different evidence and, often, a different unit in charge. The practical result is simple to state and hard to do: every obligation needs a named owner and a record that an auditor can check. The next question is how those rules are made in the first place.

### Rules are negotiated before they are implemented

Many managers meet IMO rules only when they arrive as obligations. By then the room for influence has passed. Most technical amendments to IMO conventions enter into force through tacit acceptance: unless enough Parties object, they become binding on a set date. A State that does not follow the negotiation may find itself bound by text it never examined.

[[atlas:02]]

The figure links the jurisdictional order of the oceans with implementation, enforcement and stakeholder analysis. Read as a manager, it says that negotiation and implementation are the same cycle. A delegation that knows its own implementation capacity negotiates better; an administration that has followed the negotiation implements faster. The result is a recommendation to connect the international affairs unit and the technical departments before each committee session, not after it. To do that, one has to understand the institution where the negotiation takes place.

### Knowing the institution behind the rules

IMO is often described as a regulator. It is more accurate to call it a forum in which Member States adopt rules that they then enforce themselves. Its Assembly of all Member States, its Council, five committees and seven sub-committees each play a different part in that process.

[[atlas:03]]

The practical point is that an issue rarely appears suddenly. It usually passes through a sub-committee, a committee and sometimes the Assembly over several years. An organisation that tracks the agenda of MSC, MEPC, FAL and the relevant sub-committees can see change coming and budget for it. Knowing the institution, however, is not the same as implementing what it adopts.

### From convention to evidence of compliance

Ratification is a promise. Implementation is a chain of national law, institutional responsibility, procedures, surveys, certificates, inspections and records. Since 1 January 2016 the IMO Member State Audit Scheme has used the III Code, adopted by Resolution A.1070(28), to test whether that chain exists.

[[atlas:04]]

The figure follows four phases from the legal foundation to port State control. Its managerial lesson is that delegation does not remove responsibility: a flag State that relies on recognised organisations for surveys must still supervise them. The practical result is an evidence file for each mandatory instrument, prepared long before an audit is announced. Global rules, however, only become real when they reach operations.

## Part 2: when regulation meets operations

### A single window is a governance project

The FAL Convention has required public authorities to exchange ship clearance data electronically through a single window since 1 January 2024. Many organisations treated this as a software purchase. The result in some ports was a portal that sends users to several other systems to enter the same data again.

[[atlas:05]]

The figure presents the single window as a sequence of legal mandate, data model, inter-agency governance, cyber risk and phased delivery. The managerial reading is that each data element needs an owner who can confirm and correct it, and that disagreements between agencies need a forum with the authority to decide. The practical result is measurable: fewer repeated data requests and more predictable clearance times. Once data flows, the next operational test is the contract.

### Where contract risk is really decided

When a port becomes unsafe, a voyage is delayed or a concessionaire underperforms, the question of who pays has usually been decided long before, in the tender documents and the contract. Problems often start with an unclear scope of work or a risk given to the party least able to control it.

[[atlas:06]]

The figure combines standard contract forms, port governance models, risk allocation and regulatory controls. The lesson for managers is that standard forms such as those of BIMCO are a starting point, and that the real risk lies in amendments, rider clauses and the authority's capacity to monitor performance. The practical result is a risk allocation matrix agreed before the tender is launched. Even so, some disagreements will still arise.

### Preventing disputes with better evidence

Many maritime disputes turn on the quality of the technical evidence and the independence of the person who assesses it. An expert who drifts into advocacy weakens both.

[[atlas:07]]

The figure separates the ethics of expert work, a method for building technical evidence and a matrix linking disputed issues to the documents that resolve them. For a manager, its message is to keep records as if they will one day be examined, and to bring in independent expertise early, when a disagreement can still be settled on the facts. Rules, data, contracts and disputes are national matters, but many of the solutions require partners abroad.

## Part 3: the role of international cooperation

Memoranda of understanding are easy to sign and hard to bring to life. Many end when the people who signed them move on, because no one was made responsible for delivering them and no budget or indicator was attached. Before choosing a partner, an organisation needs to know what problem cooperation should solve and what it can contribute.

[[atlas:08]]

The figure follows cooperation from the choice of model and the screening of partners to a formal agreement, accountability and performance indicators. Its managerial lesson is that an agreement creates value only when its commitments have an owner, resources and a review cycle. The practical result is a short implementation plan attached to every MoU. Cooperation is also the only way to deal with the largest shared challenge facing shipping: climate change.

## Part 4: the pressure of climate transition on decisions

### Telling binding rules from targets and drafts

Shipping now faces two layers of climate regulation at once. Global IMO measures apply worldwide, while regional European rules already put a price on emissions. On top of this sits a global framework that is still being negotiated. The most expensive mistake is to treat these three as if they had the same legal status.

[[atlas:09]]

The figure separates what is in force, what is a strategic target and what is still a draft. The carbon intensity measures EEXI and CII have applied under MARPOL Annex VI since 2023. The 2023 IMO Strategy sets a goal of net-zero emissions by or close to 2050, with indicative checkpoints for 2030 and 2040. The IMO Net-Zero Framework was approved at MEPC 83 in April 2025, but its adoption, planned for October 2025, was postponed and discussion continued in 2026. The practical result for a board is a regulatory register in which each measure carries its legal status and its date.

### What climate rules cost and who carries it

Once the legal status is clear, the financial question follows. Under the EU Emissions Trading System, shipping companies surrender allowances for 40% of their 2024 emissions, 70% of 2025 emissions and all emissions from 2026. FuelEU Maritime requires the greenhouse gas intensity of the energy used on board to fall by 2% from 2025, rising to 80% by 2050.

[[atlas:10]]

The figure translates the timeline into exposure by segment and into a board action plan. The lesson for managers is that carbon costs move through contracts: charter parties need clear clauses on who pays and who controls the operational choices that drive emissions. The practical result is to separate no-regret actions, such as data quality and energy efficiency, from investment decisions that should wait for the global framework to settle. All of this depends, in the end, on people.

## Part 5: why all of this needs education

Every rule in the atlas is applied by someone: an inspector, an auditor, a master, a contract manager, a negotiator. When their training is generic, the organisation's capacity depends on a few experienced individuals and disappears when they leave.

[[atlas:11]]

The final figure shows how training can be built around measurable competence, through clear outcomes, aligned teaching activities, assessment that tests what matters and an institutional quality system of the kind required by STCW Regulation I/8. For a manager, the message is that capacity building is part of governance, not an afterthought. The practical result is a training plan in which every course ends with an output the organisation can use.

## Conclusion

Read together, the eleven frameworks describe one system. Jurisdiction defines responsibility; institutions and implementation turn rules into evidence; data, contracts and expertise make them operational; cooperation extends them across borders; climate regulation tests them financially; and people make the whole system work. Sustainable maritime governance takes shape when regulation, institutions, technology, cooperation and human capability are developed as one integrated system.
